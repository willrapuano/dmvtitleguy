A property survey answers a different question than a title search. The title search examines recorded ownership, liens, easements, restrictions, and other documents. A survey examines the land itself: where the record boundary is, where improvements sit, and whether visible conditions conflict with the recorded picture.

That distinction matters before closing. A clean title search does not prove that a fence follows the property line, that a garage stays inside a setback, or that a driveway lies entirely on the parcel. The right property survey can reveal those issues while there is still time to investigate, negotiate, or obtain underwriting guidance.

This guide explains what a title survey is, how it differs from a boundary survey, and how survey terminology differs across Washington DC, Maryland, and Virginia.

## What is a title survey?

A title survey is a survey prepared so a title insurance company can decide what it will insure about the land's physical condition. It ties the records the title company found to what exists on the ground. It generally shows:

- The parcel boundary and corners.
- Buildings and improvements, and their distance from the lines.
- Recorded easements, rights of way, and setback lines from the title commitment, plotted on the land.
- Evidence of access to a street.
- Possible encroachments in either direction, and physical signs of unrecorded uses such as drives or utility lines.

### How the title company uses it

Title insurance is mostly about the public records. A fence off the line or a shed over an easement is not in those records, so policies commonly carry an exception for matters a survey would show. First American's homebuyer guide notes that many such exceptions can be removed under the right conditions, for example when the title company receives a survey. Old Republic's endorsement guide calls this the difference between "record" and "off record" coverage.

With an acceptable, current survey, the underwriter may remove the general survey exception, or replace it with specific exceptions for the problems the survey shows, such as a fence over the line. Without one, the exception usually stays. What counts as acceptable is the underwriter's call: Stewart Title's published guidance, for example, does not require a survey for extended survey coverage on loan policies for completed one-to-four family homes, while other underwriters and policy types may require more. Ask your settlement agent early what is needed for the owner's policy.

### Title surveys and the ALTA/NSPS standard

The formal title survey is the **ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey**, built on national standards from the American Land Title Association and the National Society of Professional Surveyors. Their stated purpose is to give title insurers what they need to insure title free of survey matters, other than those the survey discloses. The current edition took effect February 23, 2026, replacing the 2021 standards (see section 3).

In residential closings here, "title survey" is often used loosely for a smaller product: a Maryland location drawing or a Virginia house location survey. Lenders and title companies frequently accept that for a house closing, but it is neither an ALTA/NSPS survey nor a boundary survey.

## Title survey vs boundary survey

The two overlap, and a full ALTA/NSPS survey includes boundary work. The difference is who the survey serves and what question it answers.

| | Title survey (ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey) | Boundary survey |
|---|---|---|
| Main question | What survey matters affect insurable title? | Where exactly are the property lines and corners? |
| Primary users | Title insurer, lender, buyer, and their counsel | Owner, buyer, neighbor, builder |
| Standards | National ALTA/NSPS standards, plus state standards (the stricter one controls) | State standards for land boundary surveys |
| Title records | Surveyor works from the title commitment and shows the recorded easements and matters | Surveyor researches deeds and adjoiners, but title research is not required |
| Typical use | Commercial purchases, development, lender and underwriter due diligence | Fences, additions, line disputes, acreage questions, residential due diligence |

"Can the title company insure over this?" is a title-survey question. "Where can I put the fence?" needs a boundary survey. A location drawing answers neither fully.

## Property survey types at a glance

| Survey or document | What it generally shows | Best used for | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Location drawing or physical-improvements survey | The parcel from record information plus visible buildings and improvements | Residential purchase or refinance screening when accepted by the lender and title insurer | It may not establish or mark the legal boundary |
| Boundary survey | Retraced or established boundary lines, corners, monuments, and relevant improvements | Fences, additions, boundary questions, acreage concerns, or a suspected encroachment | Scope must be discussed with the surveyor; title research remains separate |
| ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey | Boundary and improvement information prepared under national title-survey standards, plus selected optional Table A items | Commercial property, development, and lender or title-insurer due diligence | The client, lender, title company, and surveyor must coordinate the requested scope |
| Topographic survey | Elevations, contours, drainage features, and surface conditions | Design, grading, drainage, engineering, and development | It is not automatically a complete boundary or title survey |
| Subdivision or record plat | The recorded creation or division of lots, streets, easements, and related dimensions | Confirming the record-lot framework and development history | A recorded plat may not show current field conditions or improvements |
| Construction layout, wall check, or as-built survey | A structure's planned or completed position relative to approved plans or property controls | New construction, additions, permit compliance, and draw inspections | The name and exact deliverable vary by jurisdiction and project stage |

The labels are not interchangeable. Before ordering, describe the decision you need to make, not only the document name you think you need.

## 1. Location drawing or physical-improvements survey

This is the limited residential product buyers most often confuse with a boundary survey.

**Maryland** calls it a location drawing, and the state's surveying regulations are blunt about it. A location drawing locates buildings and other visible improvements. It is not a boundary survey and cannot be relied on to show where the boundaries are. Its only purpose is to give a lender or insurer some assurance that the improvements are on the property.

Maryland also requires a signed consumer approval form: the buyer either approves a location drawing or requests a boundary survey that marks the corners. The form says a boundary survey costs more, and that absent a problem the location drawing is all lenders and title companies require for settlement. The drawing itself must warn against using it to place fences, garages, or other improvements. See the [Maryland Minimum Standards of Practice for Land Surveyors](https://labor.maryland.gov/license/law/lscomartitle9.pdf) (COMAR 09.13.06.06).

**Virginia** uses terms such as building location survey, house location survey, or physical survey. Under [18VAC10-20-380](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/admincode/title18/agency10/chapter20/section380/), the plat shows buildings, fences near the lines, and other improvements, and anything crossing a property line must be identified and dimensioned. Boundary bearings and distances may come from the record unless a new boundary survey is done. If the plat does not include set corners, it must say "no corner markers set." If the surveyor finds large enough discrepancies, the surveyor must tell the client a boundary survey is warranted.

A location-type product can flag an apparent encroachment, setback concern, or mismatch that deserves more work. It should not be used to decide exactly where to install a fence or claim a strip of land.

## 2. Boundary survey

A boundary survey, sometimes called a property line survey, is the right starting point when the actual lines or corners matter. The surveyor researches record descriptions and adjoining parcels, evaluates field evidence, retraces or establishes the boundary, and documents the result.

Common reasons to request one include:

- You plan to install a fence, addition, pool, garage, or other improvement near a property line.
- A neighbor's fence, shed, retaining wall, or driveway may cross the line.
- The deed description, tax map, subdivision plat, and physical occupation do not appear to agree.
- Corners or monuments cannot be found.
- Acreage, access, or the shape of the parcel is important to the purchase decision.
- The title commitment lists survey exceptions that require field information to evaluate.

Virginia's boundary-survey standards require the surveyor to compare evidence from all known sources, including the field, and to note inconsistencies with adjoining parcels. Corners must be monumented with permanent markers. The same rule states it does not require the surveyor to research title or encumbrances. See [18VAC10-20-370](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/admincode/title18/agency10/chapter20/section370/).

That is why the title examiner and surveyor do complementary work. One interprets the public-record chain and exceptions; the other evaluates boundary evidence and physical conditions.

## 3. ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey

An ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey is most common in commercial transactions, development deals, and lender-driven due diligence.

The [2026 ALTA/NSPS standards](https://www.alta.org/topics/topic-land-survey-standards) took effect February 23, 2026, superseding the 2021 edition. A complete survey includes the fieldwork, a plat or map relating the results to the title documents, any requested Table A items, and the surveyor's certification. The plat must show, among other things, plottable easements, evidence of access, buildings, and potential encroachments. Where a state's standards are stricter, the stricter one applies.

Optional Table A items are not automatic. Depending on the deal, the parties may request items such as flood zone classification, zoning setback information, building dimensions, or evidence of recent earthwork. The request must name the 2026 standards and list the Table A items wanted, so coordinate the order early among the buyer, lender, title company, attorney, and surveyor.

## 4. Topographic survey

A topographic survey maps elevations, contours, slopes, drainage features, and other surface conditions. Engineers and designers use it for grading, stormwater, site planning, additions, and development.

It answers questions such as:

- How does water move across the site?
- Where are the major elevation changes?
- What existing features affect a proposed design?
- Does the project need additional grading or drainage analysis?

A topographic survey does not establish the boundary unless boundary work is expressly in the scope. Virginia keeps separate standards for topographic surveys under [18VAC10-20-382](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/admincode/title18/agency10/chapter20/section382/), a reminder that "topographic" and "boundary" describe different professional work.

## 5. Subdivision plat, record plat, and DC building plat

A recorded subdivision plat documents how a larger tract was divided into lots, streets, easements, and other areas. It is an important record source, but it is not a current field survey of a specific improved property.

In Washington DC, record plats and building plats come from the Office of the Surveyor, covered in the next section.

## Boundary surveys and plats in Washington, DC

The DC Office of the Surveyor, part of the DC Department of Buildings, maintains the District's legal records of land plats and subdivisions. According to its [FAQs](https://dob.dc.gov/node/1613861) and [services page](https://dob.dc.gov/page/surveyor-services):

- A **record lot** is a lot recorded on a subdivision plat in the Office of the Surveyor.
- A **tax lot** is created by the Office of Tax and Revenue for billing and does not change a record lot.
- A **building plat** is a scaled drawing of a lot showing lot lines and record dimensions, certified by the DC Surveyor. It is required with a building permit application for exterior work such as an addition or a deck, with existing and proposed improvements drawn on it.
- A **wall check** is a survey locating new construction on the lot. A building inspector may require one during construction to confirm setbacks, lot coverage, and no encroachments.
- **SurDocs** is the office's online search tool for its digital survey records and plats.

Two limits matter for buyers. **The office does not do field surveys**, so for a boundary survey in Washington DC you hire a licensed survey company. And **it does not record or keep private surveys** such as location drawings or ALTA surveys, so a prior owner's survey may not be available from the District.

When this guide was checked in September 2026, the office listed building plat fees of $60.50 for standard service (emailed within 3 to 5 business days) and $88.00 for expedited service (emailed by 2 p.m. the next business day). Confirm current fees on the DOB site before ordering.

A building plat shows record dimensions, not measured field conditions. Do not treat it as proof that an existing fence, wall, or addition matches the boundary.

## 6. Construction layout, wall check, and as-built survey

Construction-related surveys occur at different stages:

- **Layout or stakeout** transfers design positions to the ground before construction.
- **Foundation or wall checks** document the position of work after a critical stage, so problems are caught before the project advances. In DC, failing to meet wall check requirements can lead the Department of Buildings to stop work.
- **As-built surveys** document completed conditions for permitting, lender, owner, or design records.

For a new home or addition, these products can keep a small placement error from becoming a setback or encroachment problem. Ask the builder, lender, title company, and local permitting authority which deliverable is required and at what construction milestone.

## What a survey can reveal before closing

The survey's value is the decision it makes possible. Common findings include:

- A fence or retaining wall that does not follow the record boundary.
- A driveway, walkway, shed, deck, or garage crossing a property line.
- A structure extending into a utility easement or building-restriction area.
- No apparent legal or physical access to part of the property.
- Improvements that do not match the record description or prior plat.
- Occupation lines that suggest a possible boundary or possession question.
- Recorded easements whose field location affects the buyer's intended use.
- Missing, disturbed, or conflicting corner monuments.

Not every discrepancy prevents closing. The right response may be clarification, a revised survey, a recorded easement, a boundary-line agreement, corrective documents, an endorsement decision, a holdback, or a change to the contract. The facts, jurisdiction, lender requirements, and title underwriter all matter.

## Which property survey should you order?

Use this decision sequence:

1. **Identify the planned use.** A buyer who only needs lender and title screening has a different need than an owner planning a fence or addition.
2. **Ask the lender and your settlement agent what they require.** Do not assume the product one lender or underwriter accepts will satisfy another.
3. **Share the title commitment and relevant exception documents.** The surveyor needs the correct record material to show applicable easements and other matters.
4. **Tell the surveyor about known concerns.** Point out disputed fences, shared driveways, additions, access questions, or missing corners.
5. **Confirm what will be marked and delivered.** Ask whether corners will be set, which improvements will be located, and what format the plat will come in.
6. **Order early.** If the survey uncovers a problem, the parties need time to investigate before financing and settlement deadlines.

For commercial property, development, acreage, or a complex easement picture, coordinate the order with the title company and lender before fieldwork begins. A narrow residential product cannot simply be "upgraded" later without more research or site work.

## Property survey versus title insurance

A survey and an owner's title insurance policy solve different problems. The survey supplies evidence about boundaries and visible improvements. The title commitment and policy cover title risks subject to their terms, exclusions, exceptions, and endorsements.

A current survey may let the underwriter remove or narrow the survey exception, as described above, but the result depends on the policy, the underwriter, the jurisdiction, and the facts. Never assume a survey automatically creates coverage, or that title insurance removes the need to investigate a physical discrepancy.

## Frequently asked questions

## What is a title survey?

A title survey is a survey prepared so a title insurer can evaluate matters the public records do not show, such as boundary problems, encroachments, access, and the location of easements. The formal version is the ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey. In residential closings, people often use the term loosely for a location drawing or house location survey.

## What is the difference between a title survey and a boundary survey?

A boundary survey answers where the property lines and corners are. A title survey answers what survey matters affect insurable title, and works from the title commitment to show recorded easements and potential encroachments. A full ALTA/NSPS title survey includes boundary work; a boundary survey does not include title research.

## How do I get a boundary survey in Washington, DC?

Hire a licensed survey company. The DC Office of the Surveyor keeps plat and subdivision records and issues building plats, but it does not perform field surveys or locate property lines.

## Is a location drawing the same as a boundary survey?

No. Maryland regulations expressly state that a location drawing is not a boundary survey. Virginia also distinguishes a physical-improvements survey from a land boundary survey. If you need to establish or mark property lines, tell the surveyor you need boundary work.

## Does a title search show the property lines?

A title search may locate deeds, plats, easements, and legal descriptions, but it does not determine where the boundary and improvements are on the ground. That is survey work.

## Do I need a property survey to buy a house?

Requirements vary by lender, title insurer, property, and jurisdiction. Even when a survey is not mandatory, one can be valuable if boundaries, access, additions, fences, or future improvements affect your decision.

## What survey do I need before building a fence?

A boundary survey, sometimes called a property line survey. A location drawing may not establish the property line, and Maryland requires location drawings to say they should not be used to place fences. Confirm local permit and setback requirements before construction.

## Can a survey problem stop a closing?

It can, particularly when the issue affects access, marketability, lender requirements, or the buyer's intended use. Many findings can be resolved, insured, documented, or accepted, but they should be evaluated before settlement rather than discovered afterward.
